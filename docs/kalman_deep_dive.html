The Kalman Filter, Step by Step

An interactive deep-dive into how prediction and correction work together

The Core Idea CONCEPT

Imagine you're watching a ball fly through the air, but you can only open your eyes for a split second each time. Every glimpse (a camera frame) tells you roughly where the ball is — but your glimpse is blurry and imprecise. Between glimpses, you have to guess where the ball went based on how fast it was moving.

The Kalman filter does exactly this. It maintains two things: an estimate of the current state (position + velocity), and a measure of how uncertain it is about that estimate. Every frame, it runs a two-step cycle:

🔮
PREDICT: "Based on where I think it is and how fast it's going, it should be here by next frame." Uncertainty grows — we're guessing.
📸
UPDATE: "I just got a measurement! It says the object is over there. Let me blend my prediction with this measurement." Uncertainty shrinks — we got new info.

The genius is in how it blends: if the prediction is very certain, it mostly ignores a noisy measurement. If the prediction is uncertain (lost the object for a while), it trusts the measurement heavily. This blending weight is called the Kalman gain.

The State Vector CONCEPT

The filter's "brain" is a state vector — everything it believes about the object right now. For our 2D tracker:

state = [x, y, vx, vy]
position (where it is) + velocity (where it's headed)

Notice we never directly measure velocity — our camera only sees position. The filter infers velocity by watching how position changes over time. This is one of its superpowers: it can estimate things you can't directly observe.

Step 1: Predict PREDICT

The predict step answers: "Given what I know now, where will the object be next?" It uses a simple physics model — constant velocity — to project the state forward by one time step (dt).

x_new = x + vx × dt — position updates by velocity × time
y_new = y + vy × dt
vx_new = vx — velocity stays the same (constant velocity model)
vy_new = vy
P_new = F × P × Fᵀ + Q — uncertainty GROWS (we added Q)

The key insight: uncertainty always increases during prediction. We're extrapolating without new information, so we must become less sure. The Q matrix (process noise) controls how fast uncertainty grows. Higher Q means "I expect the object to do unexpected things" (like a rocket accelerating or changing direction).

Step 2: Update (Correct) UPDATE

When a measurement arrives, the filter compares it to the prediction and computes a correction. The size of the correction depends on the Kalman gain.

residual = measurement − H × predicted_state — how far off was our prediction?
K = P × Hᵀ × (H × P × Hᵀ + R)⁻¹ — the Kalman gain
state_new = state + K × residual — blend prediction with measurement
P_new = (I − K × H) × P — uncertainty SHRINKS (we got info)

The Kalman gain (K) is the heart of the whole thing. It's a number between 0 and 1 for each state variable:

K→0
Prediction is very certain (small P) or measurement is very noisy (large R). The filter mostly ignores the measurement and sticks with its prediction.
K→1
Prediction is uncertain (large P) or measurement is very clean (small R). The filter snaps to the measurement. This happens after losing the object — first measurement back gets high trust.
Why This Matters for Rocket Tracking CONCEPT

A model rocket can travel 50–100+ meters per second. At 30fps, that's 1.5–3+ meters between frames. Your camera might lose detection due to motion blur, sun glare, or the rocket being only a few pixels wide. Without a Kalman filter, every lost frame means total loss of tracking. With it, the filter coasts on its velocity estimate — predicting where the rocket should be even when it can't see it. When detection returns, the filter smoothly incorporates the new measurement without jerking.

In Phase 3 (gimbal control), the filter's predicted position — not the raw measurement — is what drives the motors. This gives you a smooth, continuous pointing signal even when detections are noisy or intermittent.
